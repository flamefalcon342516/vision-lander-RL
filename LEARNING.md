# LEARNING.md — Vision-Based Landing with PPO

One section per step: concepts, equations, pitfalls, interview Q&A.

---

## Step 0 — Environment, stack, architecture

### Concepts
- **Layered control / separation of concerns.** The flight controller runs the
  fast inner loops (rate ~400 Hz, attitude, velocity). The learned policy runs
  an outer loop (~10–20 Hz) that outputs velocity setpoints. This is the classic
  cascade (inner loop ≥5–10× faster than outer), and it is why sim-to-real is
  feasible: the policy sees "a system that tracks velocity with some lag", and
  that looks similar in SITL and on a real airframe.
- **Sim-to-real gap** = the difference in the transition dynamics P(s'|s,a)
  between sim and reality. A policy that commands motors faces the full gap
  (motor constants, prop wash, ESC delays). A policy that commands velocity
  faces only the residual of a closed loop, which is much smaller and roughly a
  first-order lag that system identification can capture.
- **Lockstep (SITL ↔ Gazebo).** Neither simulator advances time alone: Gazebo
  steps physics, sends sensor state (JSON, UDP 9002) to SITL, SITL computes
  motor outputs and sends them back, and only then does the next step happen. Sim
  time is therefore deterministic and can run faster or slower than wall clock.
- **Two training stages.** Stage A is a cheap approximate MDP (fast to sample).
  Stage B is an expensive faithful MDP (slow). You learn in A and then validate
  and correct in B. This is the same idea as using a model for planning in
  value iteration and then checking it against the real system.
- **Observation frame choice.** Actions are executed in a *level,
  heading-aligned* frame: ArduPilot rotates BODY_NED velocity by yaw only (source:
  `body_to_earth2D` in `GCS_MAVLink_Copter.cpp`). Observations must be expressed
  in the same frame. A body-fixed camera tilts with the drone, so marker
  positions must be de-rotated using roll and pitch.

### Pitfalls hit in this step (real, measured)
- **PyPI torch is CUDA 13 (cu130)** → needs driver ≥ 580. With driver 535 the
  install "succeeds", but `torch.cuda.is_available()` is False. Fix: the cu126
  index. CUDA 12.x minor-version compatibility lets a 12.6 runtime run on a
  12.2 driver.
- **Protobuf version clash.** The apt `gz-msgs10` Python files were generated by
  protoc 3.12. protobuf ≥ 4 (upb backend) rejects them with "Descriptors cannot
  be created directly". TensorBoard 2.21 and onnx 1.23 need protobuf ≥ 6.31, so
  downgrading is impossible. Fix: `PROTOCOL_BUFFERS_PYTHON_IMPLEMENTATION=python`.
  Measured cost: 0.28 ms to parse a 640×480 RGB image (a bytes field is
  basically a memcpy).
- **venv needs `--system-site-packages`.** The gz Python bindings are apt
  packages in `/usr/lib/python3/dist-packages`.
- **`GZ_IP=127.0.0.1`.** Otherwise gz-transport may bind to a down docker0
  interface and silently lose data.
- **ROS PYTHONPATH leakage.** Sourcing ROS adds its packages and pytest plugins
  to every Python. Unset it for this project.

### Interview Q&A
- *Why not let RL output motor thrusts?* — The policy would have to learn
  stabilisation (a solved problem), the sim-to-real gap would cover the full
  motor and aero model, and a bad action means a crash within milliseconds. With
  velocity setpoints, a bad action means a slow drift that a safety layer can
  catch.
- *What happens if your companion computer freezes in GUIDED mode?* —
  ArduPilot stops the vehicle after `GUID_TIMEOUT` (default 3 s) with no new
  setpoints. Our own safety layer adds heartbeat-loss → hover → RTL on top.
- *Why gz-transport instead of ROS 2 for training?* — Fewer hops and no DDS
  discovery. Deployment consistency comes from a camera-source abstraction, not
  from the middleware.
- *What is lockstep and why does it matter for RL?* — Sim and autopilot advance
  one step together, so results are reproducible and sim speed is decoupled
  from wall clock. Our env step must be defined in sim time, not wall time.

## Step 1 — Gazebo fundamentals

### Concepts
- **Gazebo Sim = Entity-Component-System (ECS).**
  - *Entity:* an ID (world, model, link, joint, sensor, light).
  - *Component:* data attached to it (Pose, Inertial, LinearVelocity...).
  - *System:* a plugin that runs each step and reads or writes components.
  - Physics, sensors and the services are all systems. If the system isn't
    loaded, the feature silently does nothing. Example: an IMU sensor without
    `gz-sim-imu-system` publishes nothing.
- **System update order per step:** PreUpdate (commands, e.g. our set_pose) →
  Update (physics integrates) → PostUpdate (sensors, broadcasters read the result).
- **SDF hierarchy:** `world` → `model` → `link` (rigid body) + `joint`. Each link
  has three independent parts:
  - `inertial` (mass and inertia tensor: how it moves);
  - `collision` (simple shapes: how it touches);
  - `visual` (looks only).
  Cheap collision geometry plus detailed visuals is the norm.
- **Inertia of a solid cube:** I = m·a²/6 on each axis. Wrong inertia is a top
  cause of models spinning or exploding.
- **static vs dynamic:** `<static>true</static>` = infinite mass, never
  integrated. Use it for the ground and fixed props; it's cheap.
- **World frame = ENU** (x East, y North, z Up). ArduPilot uses **NED**. The
  conversion (x↔y swap, z negated) matters from Step 2 on.
- **`model.config` + `GZ_SIM_RESOURCE_PATH`** make `model://name` URIs resolve.
- **gz-transport:**
  - Topics are publish/subscribe streams; services are request/response calls.
  - Peers find each other by multicast discovery, then exchange data over TCP.
  - Messages are protobuf (`gz.msgs.*`).
- **Physics step vs real-time factor (RTF):**
  - `max_step_size` (1 ms) sets accuracy.
  - RTF sets pacing against the wall clock: 1 = real time; larger = faster if the
    CPU can keep up.
  - The sim time is exact either way, which is what makes lockstep possible.
- **Semi-implicit (symplectic) Euler:** v += a·dt; x += v·dt.
  - After n steps a body has fallen g·dt²·n(n+1)/2.
  - Our measurement matched this to 0.1 mm (z = 2.1505 at t = 0.100 s), slightly
    more than the continuous ½gt² = 0.0490.

### Equations
- Free-fall time: t = √(2h/g) = √(2·2.0/9.8) = 0.639 s (measured 0.640 s at
  10 ms sampling; fitted g = 9.800).
- Cube inertia: I = m·a²/6 = 2·0.4²/6 = 0.0533 kg·m².

### Pitfalls hit in this step (all measured)
- **Python gz-transport services deadlock when any Python subscription is
  active.** `request()` holds the GIL; the process-wide receive thread blocks
  trying to run a subscription callback, so the response is never processed.
  - Every request timed out with a subscription active (even on another Node);
    without one, requests take 0.2 s.
  - Fix: run service calls in a separate *spawn*ed worker process.
- **RTF=0 via `/set_physics` is silently ignored** (the reply still says
  success). It does mean "unlimited" when written in the SDF. Use a large value
  (1000) at runtime.
- **`pose/info` is throttled to ~60 Hz of wall time**, whatever the sim speed.
  At 20× real time that's one pose per ~0.3 s of sim time. It's useless as
  per-step ground truth for fast RL, so Step 10 needs per-model pose publishers.
  `clock` is published on every step.
- **Clock and pose topics are independent.** Seeing sim_time = t doesn't mean
  the pose for t has arrived. Wait on the pose's own header stamp.
- **Teleporting (`set_pose`) doesn't zero velocity.** Remember this for Step 10.
- **Background processes from non-interactive shells ignore SIGINT.** Install
  explicit SIGINT/SIGTERM handlers.
- **`pkill -f <pattern>` can match your own shell's command line.** Kill by PID.
- **Spawn-mode multiprocessing can't re-import a `python - <<EOF` (stdin)
  script.** Run real files or modules.
- **`gz sdf -k`** catches misspelt element names, which Gazebo otherwise
  ignores silently.

### Interview Q&A
- *What's the difference between collision and visual geometry?* — Collision
  feeds the physics contact solver, so keep it simple (boxes, cylinders). Visual
  is rendering only and can be a detailed mesh. Using a mesh as a collision
  shape is slow and gives jittery contacts.
- *Why does a 1 ms physics step matter for ArduPilot SITL?* — The JSON backend
  exchanges sensor data and motor outputs every physics step in lockstep, and the
  autopilot's fast loops expect ~1 kHz IMU data. A coarse step means aliased IMU
  data and unstable control.
- *How would you run Gazebo faster than real time for RL?* — Raise the RTF or
  step manually (`multi_step`) while paused. Measure, though: the bottleneck
  quickly becomes the per-call IPC overhead (~2 ms per service round trip) and
  the sensor rendering, not the physics.
- *What does ENU vs NED mean, and where does it bite?* — Gazebo uses x East,
  y North, z Up; MAVLink/ArduPilot local frames use x North, y East, z Down.
  Mixing them makes the drone fly mirrored or upside-down in altitude.

## Step 2 — ArduPilot SITL + ardupilot_gazebo

### Concepts
- **SITL** = the real ArduCopter firmware compiled for Linux. Only the sensors
  and actuators are fake; EKF, mode logic and controllers are the same code
  that flies on the real flight controller.
- **The JSON backend (`--model JSON`)** splits the simulation:
  - SITL → Gazebo (UDP 9002): a binary *servo packet*: `magic=18458`,
    `frame_rate`, `frame_count`, `pwm[16]`.
  - Gazebo → SITL: a JSON *state* message: `timestamp` (sim time), `imu.gyro`,
    `imu.accel_body`, `position`, `quaternion`, `velocity` (all NED).
  - SITL's clock = the JSON timestamp. ArduPilot has no clock of its own here.
- **Lockstep, as implemented in `ArduPilotPlugin.cc`:**
  - In `PreUpdate`, once SITL is online, Gazebo *blocks* in a loop until a
    servo packet arrives, then applies motor forces.
  - In `PostUpdate`, it sends the new state.
  - SITL waits for that state before computing the next PWM. Neither side can
    run ahead.
  - Consequence 1: if SITL dies, **Gazebo freezes**, because the plugin
    re-sends the last state forever instead of timing out (lock_step branch).
    Measured: 1.00× → 0.00×, and back to 1.00× as soon as a new SITL
    connects. So "restart only SITL" is a viable reset for Step 10.
  - Consequence 2: before SITL connects, the plugin waits 1 ms per step for a
    packet. Measured: 0.68× real time with no SITL attached.
- **How the motors work in Gazebo:**
  - PWM 1100–1900 → normalised 0–1 → × `multiplier` (838 rad/s; negative for
    the counter-rotating props) → target rotor joint velocity.
  - A PID turns that target into a joint torque.
  - The `LiftDrag` systems on each blade produce the thrust.
  - So the "motor model" is a velocity-controlled joint plus blade aerodynamics.
- **Frames in the plugin:** `<gazeboXYZToNED>0 0 0 180 0 90</gazeboXYZToNED>`
  rotates ENU into NED, and `<modelXYZToAirplaneXForwardZDown>` flips the
  model's z-up body frame to z-down (FRD). Spawning with yaw 90° in ENU points
  the nose north, so the heading is 0.
- **ENU → NED for positions:** (N, E, D) = (y, x, −z). Verified: Gazebo pose
  mapped to NED agrees with SITL `LOCAL_POSITION_NED` to < 1 cm in N/E and
  ~5 cm in D (the difference between the vehicle's origin and the EKF origin).
- **Pre-arm checks:** GUIDED needs a position estimate. Arming is refused
  ("Need Position Estimate") until the EKF fuses GPS. This took ~40 s of sim time.
- **GUIDED velocity commands** time out after `GUID_TIMEOUT` (3 s). MAVProxy's
  `velocity 2 0 0` sends one `SET_POSITION_TARGET_LOCAL_NED` (frame 8 =
  BODY_NED, type_mask 4039 = velocity only), so the drone moves for ~3 s and
  then stops. This is the same message our policy will send, but streamed.

### type_mask 4039, bit by bit
| bits | meaning | value |
|---|---|---|
| 0–2 | ignore position x,y,z | 1+2+4 = 7 |
| 3–5 | **use** velocity | 0 |
| 6–8 | ignore acceleration | 64+128+256 = 448 |
| 9 | force (ignored by ArduPilot) | 512 |
| 10 | ignore yaw | 1024 |
| 11 | ignore yaw rate | 2048 |
Total 4039. To command yaw_rate too, clear bit 11 → 1991.

### Measurements
| Gazebo RTF / SITL speedup | wall time to arm | lockstep speed |
|---|---|---|
| 1 / 1 | 41 s | 1.0× |
| 5 / 5 | 9 s | 5.0× |
| 20 / 20 | 4 s | 12.2× |
| 100 / 100 | 5 s | 10.5× |
- With SITL in the loop, the ceiling is ~12× real time even before any camera
  is rendered. Compare ~41× for Gazebo alone. Stage A exists for this reason.
- Restarting SITL per episode costs ≥ 4 s of wall time just to re-arm, even
  at max speed. That's an input to the reset decision in Step 10.

### Pitfalls
- `sim_vehicle.py` rebuilds with waf on every launch unless `--no-rebuild`.
- SITL writes eeprom/logs/terrain into its cwd. Run it from `.sitl/`.
- `--wipe`/`-w` resets parameters to the defaults. Without it, parameters you
  changed in one session silently persist into the next.
- The `gz_frame_id ... not defined in SDF` warning comes from the upstream iris
  model and is harmless.
- `gazebo-iris.parm` enables precision landing (`PLND_ENABLED 1`, IRLock type)
  and a simulated rangefinder. In LAND with no target, `PLND_STRICT=1` prints
  "PrecLand: Failsafe Measures", then lands vertically.

### Interview Q&A
- *What is lockstep and what breaks without it?* — Physics and autopilot advance
  in alternating fixed steps keyed to sim time. Without it, any CPU hiccup
  becomes a sensor delay or a missed control update the real vehicle wouldn't
  have, and the results aren't reproducible.
- *Why is SITL more trustworthy than a hand-written drone model?* — It runs
  the actual EKF, mode logic and controllers. The policy is trained against the
  same velocity loop that will fly the real drone; only the airframe physics is
  simulated.
- *ArduPilot already has precision landing on moving targets
  (`PLND_OPTIONS` bit 0). Why use RL?* — It's the baseline we must compare
  against. The RL claim to test: better handling of marker loss, fast or
  manoeuvring rovers, and descent timing. If PrecLand matches it, that's an
  honest result to report.

## Step 3 — Downward camera and intrinsics

- **Pinhole model:** a 3-D point (X, Y, Z) in the camera's optical frame
  (x right, y down, z out of the lens) projects to pixel
  u = fx·X/Z + cx, v = fy·Y/Z + cy.
  - K = [[fx,0,cx],[0,fy,cy],[0,0,1]] is the intrinsic matrix.
  - For an ideal simulated camera, fx = fy = (W/2)/tan(hfov/2).
  - With W = 640 and hfov = 90°, fx = 320. Gazebo's `camera_info` reports
    319.999, which confirms it.
- **Camera mounting in SDF:** a Gazebo camera looks along its link's +x with
  image-up = +z. A pitch of +90° makes it look down, with image top = nose
  and image right = body right.
- **Rigid camera vs. gimbal:** a rigid camera tilts with the drone, so marker
  pixels move when the drone pitches. Perception must de-rotate by
  roll/pitch. Cheap real drones have no gimbal, so we design for that.
- **Headless rendering:** cameras need a renderer even without a GUI. Use
  `gz sim -s --headless-rendering` (EGL). The `libEGL warning: failed to create
  dri2 screen` lines are harmless here; images render.
- **Cost:** with the camera at 640×480 / 15 Hz, the lockstep ceiling drops from
  ~12× to **5.5×** real time (measured, RTF/speedup 20).
- **Including a model with its plugins:** `<include>` of iris_with_ardupilot
  under the name `iris` keeps the ArduPilotPlugin working (scoped joint names
  resolve relative to the included model). Attach extra links with a fixed
  joint to `iris::iris_with_standoffs::base_link`.
- **Pitfall:** `gz sdf -k` resolves `model://` through `SDF_PATH`, not
  `GZ_SIM_RESOURCE_PATH` (now exported in env.sh).
- **Observed:** a `takeoff 4` from the rover deck levelled off at 3.36 m above
  the EKF origin. Don't rely on takeoff altitude; the env commands velocities
  and uses ground truth.

## Step 4 — Rover with ArUco marker and motion controller

- **The rover is a moving platform, not a vehicle study.** Its motion is made
  exact: gravity off, chassis floating 2 cm above the ground (no wheel
  friction), and `VelocityControl` sets the body twist from
  `/model/rover/cmd_vel` every step.
  - Measured: 1 m/s commanded for 3 s of sim → 2.93 m. The small shortfall is
    command start-up latency.
- **One path generator for both stages** (`lander/envs/rover.py`): a
  vectorised unicycle (x, y, heading; inputs v, ω).
  - Stage A integrates it directly.
  - Stage B sends the same (v, ω) to Gazebo.
  - Types: static, straight, circle (ω = v/R), figure-8 (ω sign flips every full
    circle), random (Ornstein–Uhlenbeck turn rate and speed).
- **Exact arc integration:** with v and ω held constant over dt,
  Δx = v/ω·(sin(θ+ωdt) − sin θ) and Δy = −v/ω·(cos(θ+ωdt) − cos θ).
  The tests check that a circle closes to 1e-9 m.
- **Marker texture:** generated with `cv2.aruco.generateImageMarker`.
  - DICT_4X4_50, id 0: large cells, so it's readable from altitude.
  - A 0.50 m black square plus a 5 cm white quiet zone. The detector *needs*
    the quiet zone to find the outer black border.
  - Applied as a PBR `albedo_map` on a 1 mm visual box above the deck.
- **Ground truth:** the `PosePublisher` system with `update_frequency` 50
  publishes `/model/<name>/pose` at 50 Hz of **sim** time (measured exactly
  50.0 Hz). That makes it usable for rewards, unlike `pose/info`.
- **Inertia of a box:** Ixx = m(b²+c²)/12 and so on (chassis 40 kg: 3.59,
  5.06, 8.13 kg·m²).

## Step 5 — ArUco detection, pose estimation, frames, Kalman filter

### Concepts
- **Detection:** ArUco finds square contours, reads the inner bit grid, and
  matches it against a dictionary (DICT_4X4_50: 16 bits, large cells, robust
  at range).
- **Pose from 4 corners = Perspective-n-Point (PnP).** Find R, t such that
  K·(R·X_i + t) projects onto the detected corners.
  - For a planar square, **IPPE_SQUARE** solves it analytically from the
    homography.
- **Planar ambiguity:** every planar target has two pose solutions (the plane
  can be "flipped"). Near head-on views, which is most of a landing, they
  become nearly indistinguishable. We take every candidate and keep the one
  that faces the camera (marker +z toward the lens, R[2,2] < 0) with the
  lowest reprojection error.
- **Frame chain:** cam (x right, y down, z optical) → body FRD (x = −y_cam,
  y = x_cam, z = z_cam, plus the lens offset) → level (undo roll/pitch:
  Ry(θ)·Rx(φ)) → NED (undo yaw: Rz(ψ)).
  - Observations and actions both live in the level frame, the same frame
    ArduPilot uses for BODY_NED velocity.
  - Sanity rules (tested): nose-up pitch makes a marker below appear *behind*;
    a right roll makes it appear on the *right*.
- **Kalman filter:** the state is [rel_pos(3), rover_vel(2)], and the drone's
  EKF velocity is a known *control input*:
  - p' = [v_rover − v_drone_xy, −v_drone_z];
  - v_rover is a random walk.
  - During dropouts it keeps predicting correctly even while the drone
    manoeuvres. A "relative velocity" state can't do that.
  - The measurement noise grows with height: σ = 0.02 + 0.01·h.
  - One code path handles N filters (Stage A: thousands; Stage B and
    hardware: 1).

### Equations
- KF predict: x⁺ = F·x + B·u, P⁺ = F·P·Fᵀ + Q. Update: K = P·Hᵀ(H·P·Hᵀ + R)⁻¹,
  x ← x + K(z − H·x), P ← (I − K·H)·P.
- The steady-state rover-velocity error with 8 cm position noise at 10 Hz is
  unbiased, std 0.16 m/s (measured). It trades smoothness against how quickly
  it tracks a turning rover (`q_rover_accel`).

### Pitfalls (all hit and measured here)
- **Corner refinement matters at range.** Depth error at 10 m: NONE +5.5%,
  SUBPIX +1.8%, **APRILTAG −0.8%** (1.14 vs 0.91 ms per frame). We use APRILTAG.
- **solvePnP with IPPE_SQUARE returned a mirrored pose for an exactly frontal
  view.** Both of its solutions were the same wrong one, with 80 px
  reprojection error. Fix: add SQPnP as a candidate and select by
  facing/reprojection error.
- **Synthetic test renderer:** the OpenCV marker z axis points *toward* the
  viewer. Rendering with the identity rotation gives a mirror image, which
  ArUco (correctly) rejects.
- **The marker's yaw isn't the rover's heading** (the texture is 90° off in
  Gazebo). The landing pad is square and our action has no yaw, so marker yaw
  isn't used.
- **Detection quality metrics recorded:** RMS reprojection error (0.06 px on a
  Gazebo frame at 3.5 m) and marker side in pixels. The Gazebo frame gave
  height 3.54 m.

### Interview Q&A
- *Why use PnP rather than "pixel offset × altitude"?* — PnP gives full 3-D
  relative position, including height, from the marker alone. No rangefinder
  is needed, and it stays correct when the marker isn't centred.
- *What breaks if you skip attitude compensation?* — Every pitch or roll
  manoeuvre moves the marker in the image. The controller reacts to its own
  attitude, which creates a coupled oscillation.
- *Why put the KF in the level frame?* — The frame is gravity-aligned, so
  height and horizontal motion separate cleanly. It also matches the action
  frame, so the policy maps estimate → command with no extra rotation.

## Step 6 — Classical baseline: visual servoing + gated descent

- **Control law, acting on the same observation as the policy:**
  `v_xy = ff·v̂_rover + kp·x̂ − kd·(v_drone − v̂_rover)`.
  - The feed-forward term cancels the rover's motion.
  - The P term closes the remaining offset.
  - The D term damps the relative velocity.
- **Gated descent:** descend only while |x̂_xy| < r₀ + k·h (an alignment
  *cone*: loose high up, tight near the deck).
  - Marker lost > 0.6 s → stop descending.
  - Lost > 2.4 s → climb, but never above 4.5 m, because detection fails beyond
    6–7 m (measured) and climbing further would lose the marker for good.
- **A fair baseline needs tuning too.** A 120-trial random search in Stage A
  (full scenario mix with DR) raised success from 0.163 to 0.328, re-scored
  on fresh seeds because random search over-fits its own evaluation noise.
- **Where it fails (Stage A, DR on):** it holds 44–56% on static and straight
  paths at any speed, but collapses on curves: circle at 1.5 m/s 16%, at
  2.5 m/s 0%.
  - The KF's random-walk model of rover velocity lags a turning rover.
  - The feed-forward then points the wrong way.
- **Interview Q&A:** *Why not just a PID on pixel error?* — Pixel error mixes
  attitude, height and position. The KF estimate in the level frame separates
  them, and gives the rover velocity for feed-forward, which a pure feedback
  loop can't track without steady-state lag.

## Step 7 — RL theory: function approximation → policy gradients → actor-critic → PPO

### 1. Function approximation (why tables stop working)
- Value/policy iteration store V(s) in a table. Our state is continuous,
  13-dimensional, so there's no table.
- Instead use a parametric function V_φ(s) (a neural network) and fit it by
  regression on targets. **Generalisation** is the point: similar states get
  similar values without being visited.
- **The price:** fitting moves V everywhere at once, so the "contraction"
  guarantee of tabular VI is gone. Bootstrapped targets plus function
  approximation plus off-policy data can diverge (the "deadly triad"). PPO
  avoids most of this by being on-policy.

### 2. Policy gradients (optimise the policy directly)
- Policy π_θ(a|s): here a Gaussian. The network outputs the mean μ_θ(s), and
  log σ is a learned parameter.
- Objective: J(θ) = E_{τ∼π_θ}[Σ_t γᵗ r_t].
- **Policy gradient theorem:**
  ∇_θ J = E[ Σ_t ∇_θ log π_θ(a_t|s_t) · Ψ_t ], with Ψ_t = the return from t
  onwards (REINFORCE).
- **Intuition:** make actions that led to good outcomes more likely, in
  proportion to how good they were. No model of the dynamics is needed, which
  is why it works through ArduPilot as a black box.
- **Baseline:** subtracting any b(s) leaves the gradient unbiased and cuts
  variance: Ψ_t = G_t − V(s_t).

### 3. Actor-critic and the advantage
- **Advantage:** A(s,a) = Q(s,a) − V(s), i.e. how much better this action was
  than average.
- The actor is π_θ; the critic V_φ is trained by regression to returns. The
  critic provides the baseline (and bootstraps).
- **TD error:** δ_t = r_t + γ V(s_{t+1}) − V(s_t). This is a one-step,
  low-variance, biased estimate of A.

### 4. GAE (Generalised Advantage Estimation)
- Â_t = Σ_l (γλ)^l δ_{t+l}.
- λ = 0 gives the one-step TD error (low variance, biased); λ = 1 gives the
  Monte-Carlo return minus V (unbiased, high variance). We use λ = 0.95.
- At a **time-limit truncation** the episode didn't really end, so bootstrap
  with γ·V(s_T). SB3 does this when info["TimeLimit.truncated"] is set, and
  both our envs set it.

### 5. PPO (Proximal Policy Optimisation)
- **Problem:** a big policy step on one batch of data can wreck the policy.
  Data is collected on-policy, so a bad policy then also collects bad data.
- **Ratio:** r_t(θ) = π_θ(a_t|s_t) / π_θ_old(a_t|s_t).
- **Clipped surrogate:**
  L^CLIP = E[ min( r_t Â_t, clip(r_t, 1−ε, 1+ε) Â_t ) ], with ε = 0.2.
  - The objective stops rewarding moves beyond ±20% probability change.
  - That makes it a cheap, first-order trust region (cf. TRPO's KL
    constraint).
- **Full loss:** −L^CLIP + c_v·(V_φ(s) − V_target)² − c_e·H[π_θ(·|s)].
  - The entropy bonus (c_e) delays premature convergence. We use 0 and rely on
    the learned σ (initialised at e^−0.5 = 0.61).
- **Loop:** collect n_envs × n_steps transitions → compute GAE → K epochs of
  minibatch SGD → repeat. `target_kl` stops the epochs early if the policy
  moves too far.

### Toy run (measured)
SB3 PPO on CartPole-v1 (CPU), with mean evaluation return after each chunk:
437 @ 10k steps, 496 @ 20k, 500 (the maximum) from 40k steps onward
(`python -m lander.training.toy_cartpole`).

### Interview Q&A
- *Why does PPO clip the ratio rather than the advantage?* — The ratio
  measures how far the new policy has moved. Clipping it bounds the update size
  regardless of the advantage's scale.
- *Why is PPO on-policy, and what does that cost?* — The surrogate is valid
  only near π_old, so old data is thrown away after K epochs. That's sample
  inefficient, which is exactly why we built a 176k-steps/s simulator.
- *What does γ really set?* — The effective horizon 1/(1−γ). It has to cover
  the delay between a decision and its consequence. See the Step 9 pitfall.

## Step 8 — Stage A fast env + system identification

- **System identification:** command velocity steps in GUIDED (streamed at
  10 Hz) and fit
  `a_des = clip((u(t−d) − v)/τ, ±a_max); ȧ = clip((a_des − a)/dt, ±j); v̇ = a`.
  - Horizontal: τ = 0.35 s, d = 0.07 s, a_max = 3.0 m/s², j = 4 m/s³
    (ArduPilot `PSC_JERK_NE` = 5); RMSE 0.044 m/s.
  - Vertical: τ = 0.15 s, a_max = 1.0, RMSE 0.013 m/s.
  - Tilt = 0.96·atan(a/g).
- **Sysid found a real bug:** `WP_YAW_BEHAVIOR` (default 2) yawed the drone
  toward the direction of travel. A −3 m/s x command produced ~2 m/s of y
  velocity. Now set to 0 in `configs/ardupilot/lander.parm` (also needed on
  the real drone).
- **Why jerk matters beyond fit quality:** without it, Stage A's acceleration
  (and therefore camera tilt) stepped 15° instantly. That injected large fake
  measurement errors, and even the PID failed in Stage A while succeeding in
  Gazebo.
- **Image-time attitude:** de-rotating a 165 ms-old image with the *current*
  attitude during a 15° manoeuvre moves a marker seen from 6 m by ~1.6 m.
  - Both envs, and the runtime, now look up the attitude at the image
    timestamp (`MavLink.attitude_at`).
  - That needs one clock for camera stamps and attitude samples: sim time in
    Stage B, `time.monotonic` on hardware.
- **Detection model fitted to Gazebo:**
  - P(detect) = sigmoid((side_px − 24.5)/1.5), giving 74% at 26 px and 26% at
    22 px.
  - Plus FOV (all four corners inside the image), latency 0.02–0.165 s, lateral
    noise ∝ h, depth noise ∝ h², and a +2% depth bias (rendering shrinks the
    marker by ~0.8 px).
- **Throughput:** 1.2k steps/s at N=1, 46k at N=64, **176k at N=1024** (no
  Python loop over envs). Gazebo + SITL runs ~10 steps/s.
- **Identical spaces by construction:** both envs call `task.build_obs`,
  `task.reward`, `task.check_termination` and the same `MarkerKF` and
  `RoverFleet`.

## Step 9 — Reward design, PPO training, curriculum

### Reward terms (configs/task.yaml)
| term | form | why | trade-off |
|---|---|---|---|
| shaping | γΦ(s′) − Φ(s), Φ = −(\|d_xy\| + h + 2·\|v_rel\|·e^{−h/2}) | progress signal every step (alignment, descent, velocity matching near the deck) | potential-based, so it can't change the optimal policy (Ng 1999); the weights only change learning speed |
| marker lost | −0.05 per step not detected | keep the marker in view | too large → the policy refuses to descend into the blind last 0.5 m |
| smoothness | −0.02·\|a_t − a_{t−1}\|² | less chatter = less tilt = better detections | too large → sluggish tracking |
| time | −0.01 per step | land rather than loiter | too large → reckless dives |
| success | +50 + 20·(1 − xy_err/0.25) | the goal, plus precision | |
| miss | −10 | touched down outside tolerances | must be > timeout, or the policy never tries |
| timeout / crash / OOB | −30 | never landing is a failure (battery) | |

### Reward/MDP iterations (real, measured; kept as evidence)
1. **v1** (miss −30, timeout 0, γ = 0.99): 0% success at 3M steps. Timeouts
   rose to 99%, and the policy learned to hover. Missing cost −30 while
   hovering cost ~nothing (`results/runs/reward_v1_s0`).
2. Timeout −30 and miss −15, still γ = 0.99: still 0% at 2M. The policy
   descended at 0.1–0.2 m/s and timed out. **Cause:** γ^400 ≈ 0.02, so a
   timeout 40 s away is invisible to a 10 s horizon, while a miss is
   immediate.
3. **γ = 0.995** (20 s horizon), 30 s episodes, miss −10: the policy learns
   (0 → 40% static at 6M). But 77% of misses were sliding > 0.5 m/s, so the
   velocity-matching potential weight went 0.5 → 2.0.
4. **Curriculum with a DR ramp:** with full DR from the start, even the tuned
   PID lands only 59% on a static rover (84% without DR), so the 80% gate
   could never open. Stages now carry `dr_level`, and wind, lighting and
   noise come last. Result: 66% static by 5M, then promotion.

### PPO hyperparameters (configs/ppo.yaml)
- 256 envs × 128 steps = 32,768 transitions per update; minibatch 4096 ×
  5 epochs.
- lr 3e-4, decayed linearly. clip 0.2. GAE λ 0.95. vf_coef 0.5. Gradient norm
  0.5. target_kl 0.05.
- Separate 256×256 tanh MLPs for the actor and the critic. log σ starts at
  −0.5.
- Reward normalisation (VecNormalize). Observations are already scaled to
  ~[−1, 1] in `task.build_obs`, so no observation normalisation; that keeps
  ONNX export simple.
- **Measured:** the GPU gives 41k steps/s vs 16.6k on CPU, despite SB3's
  warning that MLP policies are usually faster on CPU. The large minibatches
  make the difference. Measure, don't assume.

### Reading TensorBoard
- `outcome/*`: fractions of finished episodes by outcome.
- `curriculum/stage`: steps up when the gate opens. A temporary success dip
  right after promotion is expected.
- `landing/*`: precision of successful landings.
- `rollout/ep_rew_mean`: **beware.** In v1 it *rose* while success stayed at
  0%, because the policy got better at the wrong thing. Always watch the task
  metric, not just the return.

## Step 10 — Gazebo Gymnasium env + reset strategy

- **Reset options (measured where possible):**
  1. Restart SITL each episode: ≥ 4 s of wall time just for EKF convergence
     even at 20–100×, plus arming and takeoff.
  2. Reset the Gazebo world: SITL isn't reset with it, and Gazebo freezes
     whenever SITL is down (lockstep).
  3. Teleport the drone: the EKF sees a position jump, and teleporting doesn't
     zero velocity.
  4. **Re-position in flight (chosen):** SITL keeps running. Stop the rover
     (teleporting the rover is fine, since nothing estimates its state), then
     fly a GUIDED position setpoint to a random start. **Measured: 0.7–1.2 s
     wall per reset at 5×.**
- **Virtual touchdown:** the episode ends a few cm above the deck, so there's
  no physical contact, flipping or disarming. After the episode, the env
  immediately commands a climb.
- **Hard reset** if the drone flips or disarms: remove the drone *while SITL
  still runs* (otherwise lockstep freezes Gazebo), stop SITL, respawn, restart.
- **Timing:** one env step = 0.1 s of sim ≈ 21 ms wall at 5×, i.e. ~10× slower
  than one Stage A env, and ~20,000× slower than vectorised Stage A.
- **Ground truth for reward/metrics:** sim-time `PosePublisher` topics. The
  policy only ever sees the camera → ArUco → KF estimate.
- **Detection range** (0.5 m marker, 640 px, 90° FOV): 100% ≤ 5 m, 74% at 6 m,
  26% at 7 m, 0% ≥ 8 m. Start heights were set to 3–6 m accordingly. A real
  system would add a larger outer marker for high-altitude acquisition.

### Step 9 continued: why PPO from scratch failed, and residual RL (the decisive iteration)
These measurements are the core research finding of the project.

| Attempt | Change | Result (Stage A) |
|---|---|---|
| v1 | miss −30, timeout 0, γ 0.99 | 0% success: hovers to timeout |
| v2 | timeout −30, miss −15 | 0%: descends at 0.1–0.2 m/s and times out (γ^400 ≈ 0.02 hides the timeout) |
| v3 | γ 0.995, 30 s episodes, phi_v 2 | 66% static, then **0% on moving rovers** at stage 1 for 5M steps |
| v4 | graded miss penalty | same: dives at full speed from any offset |
| v4 + cone | horizontal error weighted by 1 + 4/(h + 0.5) | same, ~0.2 on stage 1 |
| relobs | relative-velocity obs + rover-relative action (speed invariance) | static 75% → **0.1 m/s rover 4%** (PID 84%) |
| low noise | std 0.6 → 0.2, action range 4 → 2.5 m/s | tracks within 0.1 m but **never descends** (100% timeouts) |
| BC warm start | clone the PID, then PPO | static 0.93 (beats the PID), moving 0.11; under full DR 0.11 vs PID 0.29 |
| **residual** | **PPO learns a correction on top of the PID, zero-initialised** | **0.33 vs PID 0.29 at 8M steps; circle 0.27 vs 0.17, figure-8 0.26 vs 0.20, random 0.19 vs 0.13** |

**Diagnosis:**
- Landing on a moving target needs *simultaneous* precise tracking and committed descent.
- PPO found one half at a time: "dive" (works on static rovers) or "hover-and-track" (never lands).
- Each is a local optimum, and the joint behaviour is rarely reached by Gaussian exploration.
- A curriculum that starts static actively *taught* the dive and then failed to unlearn it, an example of catastrophic interference.
- **Isolation test:** from a behaviour-cloned start on a fixed 0.4 m/s scenario, PPO improved 0.47 → 0.67, while lr = 0 stayed at 0.47. So PPO works on moving rovers; the problem was the starting basin.

**Residual policy learning:** a = clip(a_PID + 0.5·a_res).
- The residual's output layer starts at zero, so training begins *exactly* at the PID, with no imperfect clone.
- The network only has to learn where the PID is wrong (turns, fast rovers). That's a smaller, better-conditioned problem.
- It's deployed the same way: numpy PID + ONNX residual in the runtime.

**Interview Q&A:**
- *Isn't residual RL "cheating" against the PID baseline?* — No. It's the honest version of the claim: RL improves a strong classical controller, and the comparison isolates exactly what learning adds. The from-scratch attempts are reported as a negative result with evidence.
- *How do you detect a local optimum rather than slow learning?* — Look at the outcome mix and behaviour, not the return:
  - success flat for millions of steps;
  - all failures of one type (timeouts, or misses at a characteristic distance);
  - an isolation experiment on a fixed scenario.

## Step 11 — Domain randomisation (Stage A and Gazebo)
**Concept:** a policy trained on one exact simulator learns that simulator's quirks. Domain
randomisation (DR) samples the physics and sensing parameters per episode so the policy has to
work across a *range*, ideally one that contains reality.

- **Stage A** (configs/fast_env.yaml): drone response (time constant, delay, accel/jerk limits from
  the Step 8 sysid ± margins), OU wind gusts, detection range shift (lighting/blur/print), pose noise.
- **Gazebo** (configs/gazebo_env.yaml `dr:`): steady wind 0–4 m/s plus OU gusts applied as a drag
  force on the drone base link (F = k|v_air|v_air), sun intensity 0.35–1.0 and elevation 25–90°.
  Off for plain evaluation, so Gazebo numbers are on the nominal world.

**Measured (ablation `no_dr_s0`, Step 12):** training *without* DR scored 38% on the DR test set,
the same as the DR-trained seeds (37–38%). In this project DR did not buy robustness. Likely
reason: the residual sits on a PID that already absorbs most parameter variation, so the residual
has little DR-specific to learn. Reported as a null result.

**Interview Q&A:**
- *When does DR fail?* When the randomised range does not cover the real gap (the Stage A → Gazebo gap
  in Step 12 may lie in parameters we did not randomise, e.g. touchdown contact; not yet tested), or when
  the range is so wide that the best policy is overly conservative.

## Step 12 — Evaluation protocol and results
**Protocol** (lander/eval/evaluate.py): a fixed grid of 13 cells — static + {straight, circle,
figure-8, random} × {0.5, 1.5, 2.5} m/s — fixed seeds, Wilson 95% CIs, outcomes split into
success / miss / crash / out of bounds / timeout. Stage A: 500 episodes per cell per policy
(1000 for the PID). Gazebo + SITL: 3 per cell at 5× real time.

| Controller | Stage A, full DR | Stage A, nominal | Gazebo (n=39) |
|---|---|---|---|
| PID (tuned) | 31% | 48% | 54%, 7.3 s, 4.8 cm |
| residual PPO s0 / s1 / s2 | 38% / 37% / 38% | 51% (s0) | 51%, 4.7 s, 11.8 cm |
| curriculum s0 | 36% | 48% | – |
| no DR s0 | 38% | 50% | – |

**Reading the results:**
- Stage A: the residual wins on every seed, mainly on curves at 1.5 m/s (circle 28% vs 18%,
  figure-8 30% vs 17%) and lands ~35% faster. Nobody solves curves at 2.5 m/s (≤ 2%); a likely
  (untested) cause is the drone's identified acceleration/jerk limits versus the rover's turn rate.
- Gazebo: **no measurable gain**. Both controllers land every low-speed cell and fail the fast
  curves; the residual lands faster but less centred. The sim-to-sim scatter
  (docs/figures/sim_to_sim.png) shows Gazebo is *more* bimodal than Stage A: easy cells are easier,
  hard cells are harder.
- Curriculum: slightly worse on curves (circle 1.5: 15% vs 28%), consistent with Step 9's
  finding that starting static teaches a "dive" habit.

**Interview Q&A:**
- *Why report a CI and not just a mean?* With 3 episodes per cell a Gazebo cell at 100% has a CI
  of 44–100%; two controllers at 51% and 54% over 39 episodes are statistically indistinguishable.
- *Why is the Stage A success rate lower than Gazebo's?* Stage A's grid uses full DR; Gazebo is
  the nominal world, and the two simulators differ. Compare controllers within a column, not across.

## Step 13 — Deployment runtime (summary; code in lander/deploy/)
- `export.py`: ONNX actor; parity with SB3 to 1e-4; **0.021 ms mean, 0.044 ms max** per inference on
  one laptop CPU thread (re-measured for the final residual policy).
- `runtime.py`: fixed 10 Hz loop, newest-frame camera thread, attitude at image time, numpy PID +
  ONNX residual, latency budget logged per tick, motor cut on the deck.
- `safety.py`: a separate filter, unit-tested exhaustively — pilot override → go silent, heartbeat
  loss, NaN, geofence, marker lost → hover then RTL, ceiling, blind-descent floor, speed clamps.
- `rehearsal.py`: runs the *real* runtime against Gazebo + SITL over the serial-port MAVLink path.
- **Concept:** the learned policy is never trusted alone; the safety layer has veto power and is
  much simpler than the policy, so it can be tested exhaustively.

## Step 15 — Pixel student via DAgger (stretch)
**Concept:** the teacher (residual PPO) sees the marker *pose*; the student sees only *pixels*.
Plain behaviour cloning fails because the student's small errors take it to states the teacher
never visited (compounding error). **DAgger** fixes this: roll out a mix of teacher and student
(β = 1, 0.5, 0.25, …), label *every* visited state with the teacher's action, aggregate, retrain.

- Student: a small CNN on two stacked 96×72 grey frames + drone velocity/attitude; 5 iterations ×
  38 400 samples (192k total, ~2.6 GB as uint8).
- **Result: 34.9% under full DR (n=5200) vs teacher 38% and PID 31%** — ~90% of the teacher.
  Same failure shape as the teacher: fails on fast curves (timeouts dominate at 2.5 m/s).
- **Caveat:** frames come from a synthetic renderer in Stage A (lander/stretch/pixels.py), not the
  Gazebo camera. This shows the policy is learnable from pixels; it is not a Gazebo-validated
  pixel controller.
- Engineering note: the first run froze the laptop at iteration 3. Each iteration kept the
  sample list, the previous concatenated tensor and the new one (~3 copies); freeing the old tensor
  first cut the peak to two copies and the rerun stayed at 4.5 GB.

## Step 16 — Honest summary
- **Built:** a full Gazebo + ArduCopter SITL pipeline, ArUco → PnP → Kalman perception, a fast
  identified simulator, PPO training, a deployment runtime with a safety layer, and a pixel student.
- **Found:** PPO from scratch falls into dive / hover local optima on moving targets; residual PPO
  on top of a PID fixes that and improves Stage A success by ~7 points (3 seeds) and landing time by
  ~35%. That gain **did not transfer** to Gazebo (51% vs 54%, n=39) — the main open problem.
- **Next:** train or fine-tune the residual in Gazebo (or fit Stage A to Gazebo's touchdown
  behaviour), add a precision term to the reward, and render student frames from Gazebo.
